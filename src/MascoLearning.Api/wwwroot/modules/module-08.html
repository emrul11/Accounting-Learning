Payroll & Labor Costs

"Two workers earn the same ৳15,000. One tee absorbs part of that money as its cost — the other worker's salary never touches any garment. Which is which, and why does it decide your prices?"

The Mystery

Payroll day. Mizan processes salaries for a sewing operator (৳15,000) and an accounts assistant (also ৳15,000). Same amount, same bank run, same day.

Yet in the costing sheets, the sewing operator's wage appears inside every tee she made this month — while the accounts assistant's salary appears nowhere near any garment. The new intern objects: "That's unfair! Both are employees! Why do only SOME salaries count as garment cost?"

The answer is one line: direct vs indirect labor. Draw it wrong and every price Masco quotes is quietly wrong too.

 ★ YOU ARE HERE: the fork that splits wages into product cost vs period cost.

Payroll is one of Masco's biggest cash outflows AND the most misclassified cost in manufacturing. One salary sheet feeds three different destinations — product cost, factory overhead, or admin expense — depending on WHOSE hands and WHERE they work.

The Lesson: The Fork

Rule 1 — Direct labor goes INTO the product

If you can point at the garments a person touched (sewing operators, knitting machine operators on orders), their wages pour into the WIP buckets from Module 5. Their hours ARE the "direct labor" ingredient of product cost.

Rule 2 — Factory-support labor becomes OVERHEAD

Supervisors, line chiefs, QC inspectors, mechanics, storekeepers: essential, but you can't say "this supervisor spent exactly 7 minutes on PO-2291." Their salaries join the factory overhead pool, applied to products through drivers (Module 5). Still product cost — but shared, not traced.

Rule 3 — Company-support labor is ADMIN/PERIOD expense

Accounts staff, HR, IT, management, sales office: these salaries never touch garments. They are expenses of the MONTH, not of any unit produced — they hit the P&L when incurred, regardless of how many tees shipped.

Beyond gross salary: the full cost stack

A worker's real monthly cost ≠ take-home pay. The employer adds: attendance/festival bonus provisions, overtime premiums, group insurance, and eventually WPPF (Workers' Profit Participation Fund — legally required share of profit, provisioned annually). The payroll system must accrue these WHEN EARNED, not just when paid — accrual again (Module 6's heartbeat applies to people too).

Overtime must survive cross-examination

Module 0 promised this: overtime records get tested against production output. 50 operators × 40 OT hours with NO matching output jump = either false attendance, broken machines burning hours, or fraud. Finance flags it; HR explains it; both documents must agree because BOTH feed the books.

There are no dumb questions
A quality inspector touches every garment. Isn't he DIRECT?
Touch isn't the test; TRACEABILITY is. Can you measure his time attributable to PO-2291 specifically? No — he serves all orders simultaneously. Shared service = indirect = overhead pool. The test is attribution, not proximity.
If we classify more people as direct, product cost rises — why would anyone want that?
Classification isn't a choice of convenience; it's truth about how cost behaves. Underpricing happens when direct costs hide in overhead pools and vice versa. Correct classification makes each decision use the right number.
We PAY salaries after month-end. When are they EXPENSED?
When EARNED — the month workers worked. Payday creates a payable→bank entry later; the salary expense and its WPPF/bonus provisions belong to August even if cash leaves in September. Accrual accounting, always.

Your Turn

Exercise 1 — Route the salary

Direct labor / Factory OH / Admin? Decide before revealing:

  1. Knitting operator on order POs
  2. Dyeing floor supervisor
  3. Compliance officer (BSCI/audit liaison)
  4. Sample-room tailor making buyer prototypes

Solutions to Exercise 1:

  1. Direct labor — traceable hours on specific orders → WIP—Knitting.
  2. Factory OH — supervises all dyeing work; can't be attributed per order.
  3. Admin — company-level function; period expense.
  4. Tricky! Prototypes for specific buyer negotiations are usually charged to sampling/R&D cost — often treated as period expense unless billable to the order. Key lesson: some roles need a POLICY decision, documented once and applied consistently.
Exercise 2 — The impossible overtime

Sewing line 4 reports 30 OT hours per operator this month, yet its output FELL 10% versus last month. List what could explain it, and which documents finance will pull first.

Solution to Exercise 2:

Possible explanations include: machine breakdowns forcing slow manual work, rework waves from a bad fabric batch, false/padded attendance, training downtime, or style complexity change. Documents pulled first:

  • Production output logs per line/day (the denominator)
  • Attendance + OT authorization records (who approved, biometric data?)
  • Maintenance/downtime logs (mechanical cause?)

Whatever the truth, the COST of those OT hours still flows into WIP—Sewing — but unexplained overtime without matching output is exactly the inconsistency Module 0 warned about, and auditors sample precisely these months.
