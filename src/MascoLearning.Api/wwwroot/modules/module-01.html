Double-Entry Bookkeeping

"Why does every transaction have two sides — and why must they always, always balance?"

The Mystery

Rafiq, our newest developer, wrote a quick test last night. He posted a trial journal entry into the staging system: "Inventory—Yarn increased by ৳31,00,000". One line. Simple. Done.

The system rejected it with an error he'll never forget: "Entry out of balance. Posting refused."

Rafiq is annoyed. The amount was typed correctly! There was nothing mathematically wrong! So why did the machine refuse a perfectly good number? Solve his puzzle and you have understood the most successful data model in human history — one older than the printing press in English.

The Lesson: Two Sides to Every Story

Here's the secret. When Masco received ৳31 lakh of yarn, what actually happened? Two things at once:

  1. Masco gained something — yarn worth ৳31 lakh.
  2. Masco became obligated — it now owes ABC Yarn ৳31 lakh.

One event. Two consequences. That's not accounting's choice — that is reality. Every business event changes at least two things: you get something from somewhere, or give something to somewhere. Double-entry bookkeeping simply refuses to lie about this. It writes down BOTH sides, every time.

The two sides get old Italian names:

  • DEBIT (Dr) — the left side. Where value came to rest.
  • CREDIT (Cr) — the right side. Where that value came from.

Forget the everyday meanings of these words ("credit" is NOT "something good", debit is NOT "something bad"). In accounting they mean exactly one thing: left side and right side. Like stage left and stage right. 

        THE LAW OF THE JOURNAL ENTRY

        Total DEBITS  ≡  Total CREDITS     (for every single entry)

        If they differ by even ৳1 → the system refuses to post.
        Rafiq's error: he told half the story (got yarn),
        and hid the other half (owes supplier).

The words you need first

Before any rules, six words. Everything else in this course is built from them.

Account
A named bucket that collects one kind of change. "Inventory—Yarn" collects every change in the yarn we own. Each bucket gets a balance: the accumulated total of its changes, sitting on one side.
Asset (what we own)
Things that hold value for the business: yarn in the warehouse, cash at City Bank, machinery, money H&M owes us.
Liability (what we owe)
Amounts we must pay others: the debt to ABC Yarn for Tuesday's yarn, VAT we collected for NBR, bank loans.
Equity (the owners' stake)
What is left of the assets after every liability is paid: the share capital Masco's owners put in, plus profit kept in the business over the years.
Revenue (value earned)
What the business earns by doing its job: exporting garments to H&M.
Expense (value used up)
Costs consumed while earning that revenue: electricity for the dyeing hall, salaries, freight. Buying yarn is NOT an expense yet — it only becomes one when the yarn is used.

Two quick checks before moving on. First: assets, expenses, liabilities, equity, revenue — five types, and every account in the entire chart of accounts (Module 2) belongs to exactly one of them. Second: profit is not a sixth type. Profit is simply revenue − expenses, and it lands inside equity.

Where do debits and credits increase things?

Here is the part that trips everyone up. Left/right does not mean increase/decrease. Each account type has its own natural side:

Account typeExamples at MascoIncreased byDecreased by
Assets (what we own)Yarn inventory, machinery, cash at bank, H&M receivableDEBITCREDIT
Expenses (value used up)Electricity bill, overtime, glue purchaseDEBITCREDIT
Liabilities (what we owe)ABC Yarn payable, LC payable, VAT payableCREDITDEBIT
Equity (owners' stake)Share capital, retained profitCREDITDEBIT
Revenue (value earned)Export sales to H&MCREDITDEBIT
Memory trick

DEA: Debits increase Expenses and Assets. Everything else (Liabilities, Equity, Revenue — LER) increases by credit. Say it ten times: "DEA-LER." It will save you a hundred times in this course.

Mystery solved

Rafiq's one-line entry said only "Inventory—Yarn increased" — half of a real event. The yarn arrived and a ৳31,00,000 debt to ABC Yarn was born. A single-sided entry cannot say that, so the posting engine refused it. His number was fine; his story was incomplete. To post it, he needed a second line: credit Accounts Payable—ABC Yarn ৳31,00,000. The next section builds exactly that entry, with all the reasoning.

There are no dumb questions
If debits are on the left, does a debit mean "money coming in"?
No! Look at Tuesday's entry: the debit was inventory (goods came in), but if Masco PAYS cash for electricity tonight, the debit is the expense while cash (an asset) gets the credit and goes DOWN. Debit/credit is position, not direction of money.
Can one entry have three lines? Five?
Yes — as long as total debits equal total credits. Paying workers' salaries in cash and partly withholding tax can easily touch four accounts at once. Two lines minimum; no maximum.
Who invented this? Was it an accountant?
A Franciscan friar and mathematician, Luca Pacioli, described it in Venice in 1494 — five centuries ago. Venetian merchants used it even earlier. It survived because it works: every entry has two equal sides, so the books stay in arithmetic balance. But be careful what that balance proves — and what it does not. A single-sided entry breaks the balance and is caught immediately; an omitted entry, a duplicated one, or one posted to the wrong account can still balance perfectly. Balancing is an arithmetic check, not a truth check — later modules build the controls that catch those.

Exercise 2 — Solve Rafiq's puzzle

In your own words (try writing one sentence before revealing): why did the system refuse Rafiq's single-line entry of ৳31,00,000?

Solution to Exercise 2:

Because the entry recorded only one of the two real consequences of the event. The yarn arrived AND a debt was born. A valid entry needs both sides — debits equal to credits — so refusing single-sided entries is how the system enforces reality.

How the practice steps work

The guided exercises give you the account types — you supply the direction and the sides. The independent exercise removes those clues. The variations change one fact, because understanding means you can adapt the rule when the facts change. Try each answer before revealing it.

Exercise 3 — Guided receipt: the account types are supplied

On 10 April, Masco receives dyes worth ৳9,00,000 from Colorchem on 30-day credit for the dyeing floor. The dyes go into stores, ready for production. Inventory—Dyes is an asset; Accounts Payable—Colorchem is a liability. For each account, decide whether it increases or decreases, then choose debit or credit. Write the complete entry before revealing the answer.

Guided answer:

The dyes arrived and are stored for future use, so the asset increases. An asset increase is a debit. The 30-day credit means Masco now owes Colorchem, so the liability increases. A liability increase is a credit.

10 April: Dr Inventory—Dyes ৳9,00,000 / Cr Accounts Payable—Colorchem ৳9,00,000.

Why not Cash at Bank? No money has moved yet — the 30-day credit means payment comes later. Crediting Bank would invent a cash outflow that never happened. Why not Dyes Expense? The dyes are stored, not consumed. They become expense (through production cost) when used, not when bought.

Exercise 4 — Guided expense: an office service

On 15 April, Masco pays ৳30,000 from City Bank for the head office's internet and phone for April. Office Utilities Expense is an expense; Cash at Bank is an asset. Decide the direction of each, choose debit or credit, and write the entry.

Guided answer:

The service was consumed during April, so the expense increases. An expense increase is a debit. Money left the bank the same day, so the asset decreases. An asset decrease is a credit.

15 April: Dr Office Utilities Expense ৳30,000 / Cr Cash at Bank ৳30,000.

Why not an asset? Unlike stored yarn or dyes, an internet service cannot be stored for later — its value is used up as it is delivered. Compare this with the factory electricity in the worked example: office services are always period expenses, while factory electricity is a special case Module 5 revisits (it becomes part of product cost there).

Exercise 5 — Independent: a new purchase

On 6 April, Masco buys generator diesel for ৳80,000 on 15-day credit from Rahman Fuels. Assume the diesel is stored for use and has not yet been consumed. Which accounts change? Classify them, decide the direction, and write the entry without looking back at the answer.

Independent answer:

Diesel held for future use is an inventory asset, so Inventory—Fuel increases by debit. The 15-day credit creates a liability, so Accounts Payable—Rahman Fuels increases by credit.

6 April: Dr Inventory—Fuel ৳80,000 / Cr Accounts Payable—Rahman Fuels ৳80,000.

Why not Diesel Expense? The fact pattern says it is still stored. It becomes an expense when consumed, subject to the company's costing policy. Why not Bank? Payment has not happened. A wrong bank credit would invent a cash outflow.

Exercise 6 — Variations: change one fact
  1. What changes in Exercise 5 if Rahman Fuels is paid immediately?
  2. What happens if half the yarn from Tuesday's GRN is returned next week, before ABC Yarn is paid?
  3. Which of these changes the debit account: paying now, or returning half the yarn?

Variation answers:

  1. The debit remains Inventory—Fuel ৳80,000. Replace the credit to Accounts Payable with Credit Cash at Bank ৳80,000 because the asset was paid for immediately.
  2. Before payment, return half the yarn by reversing half of the original receipt: Debit Accounts Payable—ABC Yarn and Credit Inventory—Yarn for ৳15,50,000 (2,500 kg × ৳620). The original receipt stays in the audit trail; the return is a new event.
  3. Paying now changes only the credit account. Returning yarn changes both the payable and inventory amounts because the business no longer owns that half.
A balanced entry can still be wrong

Someone records the diesel purchase as Dr Repairs Expense ৳80,000 / Cr Accounts Payable ৳80,000. The entry balances. What is wrong? It puts an asset into an expense account, so profit is understated now and the fuel stock is missing. The arithmetic check passes; the business meaning does not. That is why source documents, account design, and reconciliation matter.

Worked Example: The Full Life of ৳31 Lakh of Yarn

Let's follow Tuesday's yarn through THREE events. Watch how each event creates its own balanced entry, and how accounts carry balances forward like buckets being filled and emptied.

Our starting point. To see movements clearly we need opening balances. At the start of this stretch, Masco holds Cash at Bank—City Bank ৳50,00,000 (a debit balance — money we own) and nothing else: no yarn, no debts, no sales yet. Keep those two facts in view; every number below flows from them.

Event 1 — Yarn arrives (Tuesday, GR#GR-2026-0441)

What happened? Five thousand kg of yarn from ABC Yarn arrived at the gate, was counted against the purchase order, and Jamal signed GRN GR-2026-0441. ABC Yarn's invoice matches the GRN; payment is due next month. (This example ignores VAT and foreign exchange to keep the first pattern clean — Module 7 and Module 14 add them back.)

Before and after. Before: no yarn, no debt to ABC Yarn. After: ৳31,00,000 more yarn in the warehouse, and a ৳31,00,000 debt to ABC Yarn. Two things changed. Cash did NOT change — no money moved.

Which accounts, and why? The yarn goes in Inventory—Yarn, an asset account: we own it and will use it to earn revenue. The debt goes in Accounts Payable—ABC Yarn, a liability account: "payable" means an amount we must pay someone.

Which directions? Inventory increased, and assets increase on the DEBIT side. The payable increased, and liabilities increase on the CREDIT side. So:

AccountDebit (BDT)Credit (BDT)
Inventory—Yarn (asset ↑)31,00,000
Accounts Payable—ABC Yarn (liability ↑)31,00,000

What this entry did. Assets are now 50,00,000 + 31,00,000 = 81,00,000; liabilities are 31,00,000; the difference (50,00,000) is still exactly the owners' equity. Nothing touched profit — receiving yarn is not yet an expense, because nothing has been consumed. And cash is untouched: 50,00,000 throughout. One warning for later: if the books debit Inventory again when we PAY ABC Yarn, the yarn would be counted twice. Payment changes the debt and the bank, never the inventory.

Event 2 — LC payment released to supplier (next month, Bank Advice #BA-114)

What happened? We pay ABC Yarn ৳31,00,000 from City Bank, settling the invoice from Event 1.

Before and after. The debt drops from 31,00,000 to zero; the bank drops from 50,00,000 to 19,00,000. Yarn? Untouched — it is still sitting in the warehouse, exactly where Event 1 put it.

Which accounts, and why? The debt lives in Accounts Payable—ABC Yarn (liability): paying reduces it, and decreases in liabilities are DEBITS. The money leaves Cash at Bank—City Bank (asset): decreases in assets are CREDITS. So:

AccountDebit (BDT)Credit (BDT)
Accounts Payable—ABC Yarn (liability ↓)31,00,000
Cash at Bank—City Bank (asset ↓)31,00,000

See what happened across the two entries? Inventory came in and stayed. The payable was born (credit) and died (debit) — net zero. The bank paid the price. Every account is a bucket: debits fill assets, credits empty them; credits fill liabilities, debits empty them.

Event 3 — Electricity for the dyeing hall (৳4,50,000, paid same day)

A new pattern: expense. Compare with Event 1 carefully. In Event 1 we paid nothing, but owned something more — an asset. Here we pay ৳4,50,000 for electricity that the dyeing hall consumes this month. It is not stored for later like yarn; the value is used up the moment it arrives. That difference — owning versus using up — is the whole difference between an asset and an expense. (Simplification note: this first module records factory electricity as a direct expense to keep the pattern clean. Module 5 shows that in full costing, factory electricity actually becomes part of each garment's cost through overhead absorption. Office services — like the internet bill in Exercise 4 below — are always period expenses.)

Electricity goes to Utilities Expense—Dyeing Hall (expense ↑, debit side), and cash leaves Cash at Bank—City Bank (asset ↓, credit side):

AccountDebit (BDT)Credit (BDT)
Utilities Expense—Dyeing Hall4,50,000
Cash at Bank—City Bank4,50,000

The Destination: One Transaction's Journey

You have learned the entry-level rule: debits equal credits. This continues the same March we just walked through — same ৳50,00,000 opening, same yarn purchase and payment — now with the sale side added. But where does every entry end up? Each posting quietly feeds two final reports — a Balance Sheet (what the company owns and owes at a moment in time) and an Income Statement (what the company earned and spent over a period).   2. The Income Statement is independent of cash timing. Event B recognised revenue before a single taka arrived; Event E recognised expense when the taka left.

From Lesson to Software

Before you take the quiz, write the software specification a developer would need to build the posting logic from this module. Predict first, then reveal the model answer.

1. What event triggers this posting?

A source document is approved — for example a GRN, supplier invoice, payment voucher, or payroll sheet. The business event must be real and evidenced by the document; a typed amount with no source is not enough to post.

2. Which accounts, amounts, and dates apply?

One or more debit accounts and one or more credit accounts, picked from the COA. Amounts come straight from the source document (no rounding, no invention). The posting date is the date on the document or the date of the event — NOT the system entry date, because periods must stay honest.

3. What approval and validation are required?

The entry must balance (total debits = total credits), every account must exist in the COA, and the source document reference must be attached. Maker-checker separation is mandatory — the person who prepared the entry cannot be the one who approves it.

4. What happens on cancellation, correction, or retry?

Never delete a posted entry. If the event is cancelled, post a reversal entry dated the cancellation date that references the original journal ID. If the entry has an error, post a correcting entry that reverses the wrong amount and records the right one. The audit trail must always show every original posting and its reversal.
