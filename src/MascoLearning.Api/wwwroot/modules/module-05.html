"Yarn enters at ৳620/kg. A finished T-shirt leaves at ৳390/piece. How does cost FLOW through the factory — and who keeps count?"

The Mystery

Merchandiser Arif quotes H&M: "Basic tee, FOB $2.85." H&M's counter: "$2.60 or we walk." Before accepting, someone must answer a deceptively simple question: what does this tee actually COST Masco? Not guessed — known.

The yarn alone is ৳620/kg… but then it becomes fabric (knitting + dyeing costs), gets cut and sewn (labor), consumes electricity, chemicals, machine time, supervisors' salaries. Somewhere between cotton fiber and folded T-shirt, ৳620 of yarn became some larger number per piece — but WHO added what, WHERE, and HOW MUCH?

Answer that, and you can price with confidence instead of fear. That answer is process costing.

The Lesson: Buckets and Pipes

Masco is a knit composite: one factory chain turns raw yarn into exported garments. Production flows through processes in one direction, so costing flows the same way:

  • Each process is a WIP bucket ("Work In Progress" — an inventory account for half-made goods).
  • Costs are poured in: transferred-in material, direct labor, direct materials (dyes!), and factory overhead.
  • When output leaves the process, the bucket pours its total into the next process. Cost follows the product like water follows the pipe.
  • The last bucket empties into Finished Goods. That final concentration IS the product cost.

The three ingredients poured into every bucket

  1. Transferred-in cost — whatever the previous bucket handed over.
  2. Direct costs — traceable to the product: sewing-line wages, dyes and chemicals.
  3. Factory overhead (OH) — shared factory life: electricity, supervisors, depreciation, rent. Applied by a sensible driver (machine hours, labor hours).

And the master formula every bucket lives by: 

 Cost of output  =  Cost brought in  +  Costs added here
 ──────────────     ──────────────      ─────────────────
 Units produced     same units          same units
 → unit cost accumulates step by step until Finished Goods
There are no dumb questions
Where does the electricity bill actually enter?
It never touches a product directly. It pools into overhead for the period, then is APPLIED into each bucket using the driver (e.g., machine hours). That's why meter readings and production logs matter to accounting — they decide who carries which share.
What about partially-finished fabric at month-end?
Real factories always have some. The bucket's cost splits: finished units pass downstream; unfinished units stay as ending WIP at their fair share. The math can get fancy ("equivalent units") — the principle is simply: don't give unfinished cloth full-grown costs.
Why does pricing depend on this? Can't we just check competitors?
Competitors' prices tell you the market; your costs tell you whether you SURVIVE that price. H&M's $2.60 offer is only acceptable if your true cost is safely below it — including the overhead everyone forgets.

Knitting shows: transferred-in yarn ৳62,00,000, labor ৳8,00,000, applied OH ৳10,00,000, output = 100,000 kg fabric, no beginning or ending WIP. What must fabric leave the bucket AT per kg — and if the system shows ৳7.50/kg leaving, what do you suspect?

Solution to Exercise 2:

Total in bucket = 62L + 8L + 10L = ৳80,00,000. Output 100,000 kg with no leftover WIP → fabric MUST leave at ৳80/kg. If the system shows ৳7.50, someone divided wrong OR units mismatched (kg vs lbs?) OR output was misreported — because mass conservation applies to money too: ৳80,00,000 cannot become less than itself without a leak.

Key Takeaway: Process costing is cost flowing through buckets: each process collects transferred-in + direct + applied-overhead costs and passes the total downstream. The last stop — Finished Goods — holds the true product cost that pricing decisions live and die by.
